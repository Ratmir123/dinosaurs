SCENE CONTEXT
EXACT 2 HUMANS AND 1 DINOSAUR — NO DUPLICATES: THE VET, THE GIRL, THE YOUNG DINOSAUR. A parent holds the camcorder and is never seen or heard. No other people, no other animals, no second dinosaur anywhere in the shot.
A single continuous 7-second handheld camcorder take inside a small veterinary consulting room, 2003. A young dinosaur, a few months old and the size of a small dog, is on the examination table for a check-up. She is frightened and shaking. Her girl waits beside her, the vet works, and at the end the girl wraps her arms around her.

SCALE LOCK — VISIBLE PROOF IN THE PICTURE
The young dinosaur is a few months old and about the size of a small dog — roughly fifty to sixty centimetres from nose to tail-tip, the bulk of a spaniel. She is big enough that an adult would lift her with two hands and a little effort, and small enough that she takes up only a quarter of the examination table with bare steel on both sides of her.
The proof is in the objects: standing on the table her head comes level with the girl's collarbone; the vet's open hand covers the whole top of her skull; the round head of his stethoscope sits on her chest like a coin on a saucer, covering part of it rather than all of it; and when the girl embraces her at the end, the girl's arms go right around her neck and shoulders and just meet on the far side.

CHARACTERS (no reference images — these descriptions are the identity)
THE YOUNG DINOSAUR: a young two-legged dinosaur, a few months old, about the size of a small dog — fifty to sixty centimetres nose to tail. She is past newborn but nowhere near grown, and she is built like every young animal: her HEAD AND HER FEET ARE STILL TOO BIG FOR HER BODY, which she has not caught up to yet. Her head is large and round with a high domed forehead and a short soft rounded face with almost no muzzle. Her eyes are big, round, dark and liquid, taking up much of the front of her face, with clear wet catch-lights and slow heavy lids. Tiny nostrils, a small closed soft-lipped mouth. Her neck is short and creased, her body has lengthened out of its baby roundness but still has a soft belly, her legs are longer now and end in oversized soft padded feet, and her tail is longer and thinner than a newborn's but still carried low.
Her skin is smooth, soft and almost velvety — very fine scaling that barely reads as scales at all, more like a young gecko or a soft leaf, matte with a faint sheen, gathering into gentle creases at the neck, the wrists and the knees. NOT armoured, NOT ridged, NOT pebbled, NOT crocodilian: soft and yielding everywhere, so that her body visibly compresses a little where it is touched.
Colour: a soft fresh moss green across the back, fading down through pale sage to a warm cream-pink belly and throat, with a faint dusty pink flush across the cheeks and around the eyes. Natural and living like a tree frog, never plastic or saturated.
She wears ONE soft PINK nylon collar that fits her properly, with a small metal ring at the throat and one SMALL neat satin bow — no larger than a coin — tied onto that ring. A modest little pet-shop bow, the kind tied on a puppy, flat and slightly crumpled, not a gift-wrap bow and not oversized.
Her face reads unmistakably as a young animal, not as a small adult reptile.
THE GIRL: a 10-year-old girl, thin, hair pulled back into a stub of a ponytail, in a washed-thin hand-me-down cardigan and plain trousers, scuffed shoes. Ordinary unretouched skin, a bitten fingernail, a grubby knee.
THE VET: a 55-year-old man, heavy-set, greying, in worn medical clothing gone slightly grey with washing, sleeves pushed up to the elbow. Ordinary unretouched skin, thick blunt hands.

THE SHAKING — a constant of the whole shot
She trembles the way a small frightened dog trembles, and it never stops for a single frame. It is a fast, fine, continuous shiver rather than a slow shudder: a high-frequency vibration running through her shoulders and flanks in visible waves, rippling the loose skin at her neck and throat, quivering the small bow on her collar, and travelling out to the tip of her tail. Her back legs quiver visibly under her own weight and her braced feet judder very slightly against the steel. Every time the vet touches her the shaking spikes faster for a moment and then settles back to its constant rate. It continues while she is held at the end, felt through the girl's arms.

ACTION TIMING
0.0s–0.8s — Everything is already happening. THE YOUNG DINOSAUR stands hunched on the steel, legs braced wide, shaking hard and continuously. Her big eyes are fixed on the vet hands, following them. THE VET leans over her and presses the round head of a stethoscope against her chest. THE GIRL leans in with her hands flat on the table beside her, not touching her.
0.8s–2.2s — At the cold metal she sinks lower, her back legs sliding a little on the slick steel, and lifts one oversized front foot off the table and puts it back down. She lets out one thin high whine. She does not resist and does not pull away; she only gets smaller, and her eyes never leave his hands. He lifts the stethoscope, moves it a hand-width along her ribs, and sets it down again. The camera drifts a few degrees and corrects.
2.2s–3.2s — The vet straightens and turns half away toward the cabinet behind him, his back and far shoulder blocking whatever his hands are doing. She takes two unsteady steps toward the girl, her big feet skidding outward on the metal so that she has to catch herself, then stops and stands there shaking, watching that turned back.
3.2s–3.8s — The vet leans back in over her far shoulder — his own shoulder and back stay between his hands and the camera the entire time, and nothing he is holding is ever visible. She flinches hard: one sharp jolt runs the whole length of her body, the skin twitching in a ripple along her flank, her head snapping down and her eyes screwing shut, and she lets out a thin rising squeal. The shaking doubles in speed.
3.8s–4.4s — A hard crash zoom in. The lens punches onto her head and the girl's arms, the framing overshooting past them, snapping back, the autofocus hunting through them and pulling into focus, the image smeared and soft for a fraction of a second as it lands.
4.4s–6.2s — THE GIRL leans right in and wraps both arms around her — one arm over her shoulders and one under her jaw, hands meeting on the far side of her neck — and pulls her in against her own chest, pressing her cheek down onto the top of the animal's head. The dinosaur pushes her face into the girl's shoulder and stays there. The shaking keeps going and is visible through the girl's sleeves. The small bow on the collar is squashed flat against the wool. The vet's hand comes into frame from the right and pats her twice on the rump, flat and businesslike, and withdraws.
6.2s–7.0s — Very slowly she lifts her head just enough to get one big eye clear of the girl's arm and looks out sideways, checking whether it is over. The eye is wide and wet and it moves, scanning to the right. Then she pushes her face back into the girl's shoulder and hides again. The girl keeps holding her. The camera trembles and holds.

PHYSICS
She is young and unsteady, and everything about her body says half-grown: she has poor traction on the smooth steel, her oversized feet slide outward whenever she shifts her weight, and she balances by bracing her legs wide rather than by muscle. Each flinch is one involuntary contraction that runs from her shoulders to her tail-tip and then releases back into the constant shaking.
Her soft body compresses visibly wherever it touches something: flattening slightly where she rests her weight, giving under the vet's palm, squashing gently against the girl's chest when she is held, her loose neck creases gathering when she tucks her chin.
The pink collar sits snug and turns slightly around her neck as she moves, the small bow flopping with it. Every contact between the vet's big hands and her is careful and slow.
The girl's embrace has real force in it — she pulls the animal in and braces her own feet to take the weight, and the cardigan wool gathers and pills where the face pushes into it.
Contact shadows sit tight under her feet, under the girl's arms and under the table column. Nothing floats.

The main light is flat pale daylight from the tall window at frame-left — it lands on the animal's near side and on the girl, and it is the reason the left half of the room is brighter. Above them, two fluorescent tubes add a weak overhead fill with a faint green cast and put a hard bright strip along the top of the steel table, which bounces pale light back up under her chin and throat and lights her big eyes from below. Shadows are soft, doubled and short, falling down and slightly to the right. The window itself is blown out to plain white. Exposure is set by a consumer auto-iris that favours the tiles, so faces sit slightly dark and the window is pure white. Auto-white-balance sits green under the tubes and never fully corrects. No sunbeams, no rim light, no backlight.

CHARACTER ACTING
THE YOUNG DINOSAUR: she does not understand any of this and she has worked out that she cannot get away. She is not fighting and not panicking — she is enduring, which is worse to watch. Her whole strategy is to hold still, keep her eyes on the hands, and get smaller. The two unsteady steps toward the girl are the only plan she has: go to her person. When she is finally held she stops trying entirely and just hides, and the one eye at the end is her checking whether it is safe yet. She is sincere and helpless, never comic and never performing.
THE GIRL: her entire job is her dinosaur, and she takes it completely seriously. She keeps her hands on the table beside her but does not interfere while the vet is working, because she has been told not to. The moment it is over she takes hold of her, and she does it fast and hard.
THE VET: he is doing a routine job he has done ten thousand times, and he is neither cruel nor tender about it. He is efficient, unhurried and slightly bored, and his hands are gentle only in the practical way a professional's hands are. The two pats at the end are the pats of a man moving on to the next thing. His total ordinariness is what makes the scene land.
Nobody in the frame finds any of this remarkable. Nobody performs, nobody mugs, nobody looks at the lens.
