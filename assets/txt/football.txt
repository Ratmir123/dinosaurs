EXACT 4 HUMANS AND 1 DINOSAUR — NO DUPLICATES: THE STRIKER, THE FAR KEEPER, PLAYER A, PLAYER B, THE ANKYLOSAURUS. There is exactly one dinosaur in this shot and he stays in the left-hand goal the whole time. The right-hand goal is defended by a human keeper.
A single continuous 7-second handheld camcorder take on a small fenced village football cage in the late afternoon of 2003. A player strikes the ball at the left goal, which has an ankylosaurus standing across its mouth. The animal is grazing and never looks up. His tail swats once, flat and level, and the ball goes the entire length of the pitch into the other net.

The ball leaves at ankle height on a low flat line toward frame-left. Dry dust kicks up from his planted foot. The camera is already drifting left, lagging behind the ball.
0.4s–0.9s — The ball crosses the pitch right to left, low and fast, staying below the height of a standing man. The camera chases it and overshoots slightly, then corrects. THE ANKYLOSAURUS keeps his head down in the goal mouth, jaws working sideways on grass, and does not look up. At 0.8s his hips rotate a few degrees and the tail begins to load — the base swings first while the club still drags on the ground.
0.9s–1.15s — THE TAIL STRIKE. The tail sweeps FLAT and LEVEL across the goal mouth, never rising higher than the dome of his own back, the heavy bony club accelerating through a low wide arc close to the ground and tearing a spray of grass and dirt up behind it. The club meets the ball dead-on at 1.0s: the ball crushes flat against the bone into a hard white disc for a single frame, then explodes off it back the way it came. The impact snaps the camera — the whole frame jolts once and the horizon tilts about three degrees before the operator settles it. The animal's head stays down. He keeps chewing.
1.15s–3.0s — The ball travels left to right across the entire pitch on a low flat line, rising only slightly, staying below head height of a standing man the whole way. It passes PLAYER A at 1.5s, who ducks and throws both arms over his head, and passes THE STRIKER at 1.9s, who spins on the spot to follow it. The camera whips right after the ball, lagging, catching up, the pitch smearing across the frame, the autofocus hunting and pulling. The tail behind it drops back to the ground with real weight and raises a low sheet of hanging dust.
3.0s–3.4s — A hard crash zoom in on the right goal. The lens punches from the wide onto the keeper, the framing overshooting past him, snapping back, the autofocus hunting through him and pulling into focus, the image smeared and soft for a fraction of a second as it lands.
3.4s–4.0s — THE FAR KEEPER launches sideways, fully extended, both gloves stretched out ahead of him and his body flat in the air. The ball passes a clear arm's length beyond his fingertips. A puff of dust comes off the netting.
4.0s–5.2s — The keeper stays down on the ground, props himself on one elbow, and turns his head to look the whole length of the pitch back at the animal. Behind him THE STRIKER walks two steps and stops with both hands laced on top of his head. PLAYER B simply sits down on the grass. Nobody speaks.
5.2s–5.6s — The camera whips left, fast and messy, back across the pitch, the whole frame smearing into a blur of green and fence, the autofocus lost, and lands roughly on the left goal.
5.6s–7.0s — The ankylosaurus fills the framing, still standing exactly where he started, still inside the goal with the crossbar above his back. He tears one single mouthful of grass, jaws grinding sideways once, a strand hanging from the corner of his beak. His tail flicks once against the dirt at nothing at all, lifting dust. The camera trembles on him and holds.

PHYSICS
The ankylosaurus carries enormous mass: every movement starts at the hips and arrives at the extremities late, armour plates shifting slightly against each other over the ribs as he breathes, dust sifting out from between the osteoderms when the tail lands. His four feet compress the earth and stay planted. The tail is rigid along its length and driven entirely from the base — it does not curl or coil, it swings as one stiff lever in a flat horizontal plane, and the heavy club lags behind the base through the whole arc then catches up violently at the end.
The ball behaves as a real under-inflated leather ball: it deforms visibly on both impacts, flattening against the tail club and again against the far net, and wobbles slightly in flight with a visible slow spin. Its path after the tail strike is low and nearly flat, rising only a little across the pitch.
The keeper's dive is real human physics: he loads on the near leg, launches sideways rather than upward, hangs fully extended for a fraction of a second, then falls under gravity and lands hard on his shoulder and hip, sliding on the dry earth and raising dust.
The net is loose rope netting with real slack: it takes the ball, balloons well outward, drags the top bar down a few degrees, swings twice and settles.
Grass, dirt and dust: the tail strike tears a low fan of torn grass and soil up behind the arc; the landing club raises a flat sheet of dust that hangs in the low light and drifts. Contact shadows sit tight under every foot, under the ball and under the falling body. Nothing floats.

CHARACTER ACTING
THE ANKYLOSAURUS: he is eating, and that is his entire intention for all seven seconds. He is not guarding the goal, he does not know it is a goal, and he never registers that anything happened. The tail swat is an idle reflex aimed at nothing — the same flick an animal gives at a fly — and it does not interrupt his chewing for a single frame. His head never comes up and his feet never move.
THE FAR KEEPER: he genuinely tries. He reads the ball, commits early, and gives it everything he has — and he is still nowhere near it. On the ground he checks the net first, the way a keeper does, and only then looks across the pitch to work out where that came from.
THE STRIKER: he was certain that was a goal. His hands go to his head because there is nothing else to do with them.
PLAYER A: pure self-preservation — he ducks first and thinks second.
PLAYER B: his legs stop working and he sits down.
Every human in the frame is astonished, and the animal is not. The comedy is entirely in that gap — nobody performs, nobody mugs, nobody looks at the lens.
