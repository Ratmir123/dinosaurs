EXACT 5 HUMANS AND 1 DINOSAUR — NO DUPLICATES: FOUR CHILDREN, THE OLD WOMAN, THE SAUROPOD. No other people, no other animals, no second dinosaur anywhere in the shot.
A single continuous 7-second handheld camcorder take in a residential courtyard in the late afternoon of 2003. A long-necked sauropod lies across the yard, awake and completely unbothered. Four children are using him as a slide — up the tail, along the back, down the neck into the sandpit. An old woman reads on a bench and never looks up.

PHYSICS
The sauropod carries enormous mass and is almost entirely still: only the slow swell and sink of the ribcage, the flare of the nostrils, the blink and the roll of the eye move. The hide creases and stretches over the ribs as he breathes, the deep folds opening and closing, and fine dust lifts off the whole length of him on the inhale and settles on the exhale. His weight has flattened the dirt beneath him into a shallow trough, and the ground stays compressed all around his body.
The eye is wet living tissue: a clear catch-light sits in it, the lid sweeps fully and unhurriedly on each blink, and the eyeball itself rotates in the socket to follow movement while the skull stays completely still on the ground.
The children move as real children: quick, unbalanced, over-committed. The slide is real friction — a slow start on the wide part of the neck, accelerating as it narrows and steepens, a scrabble for grip, then a hard landing that folds the legs. Clothing behaves as real fabric: the windbreaker balloons and flaps on the way down the neck, the jumper rides up when a child is dragged along the hide.

CHARACTER ACTING
THE SAUROPOD: he is awake and he is allowing this. He lay down here on purpose and he is not going to get up, and his whole performance is patience — the flat, unhurried tolerance of an old animal who has been climbed on every afternoon for years. He never lifts his head, never turns his skull and never moves a limb, but he is plainly present: his eye follows each child down the neck and back up again, he blinks on his own rhythm, and his nostrils work. He is not asleep, not drugged and not indifferent — he is watching, and he simply does not care.
THE CHILDREN: this is the ordinary thing they do after school, and their attention is entirely on the mechanics of it — where to grip, when to let go, who is next. They are not amazed by the animal any more than a child is amazed by a slide. They race the cycle, they shove for turns, and when one of them wipes out the others laugh at him rather than at the situation.
THE OLD WOMAN: she is reading, and she has read through this every afternoon for years. She never looks up, not even on the breath. Her total lack of interest is the joke.
Nobody in the frame finds this remarkable. Nobody performs, nobody mugs, nobody looks at the lens.
